MAT-001Estimate Then Measure

Family activity; adapt together · About 20–40 minutes; pause whenever useful

Begin with a question

How close can an estimate be without pretending it is exact?

Gather

Ruler, paper, pencil, three safe objects.

Before you begin: Choose accessible tabletop objects; no climbing to measure.

Predict, explore, revise

  1. Estimate each object's length and write units before measuring.
  2. Measure from the ruler's zero mark.
  3. Calculate the difference between estimate and measurement and explain one revision.

Keep your evidence

Three estimates, measurements and differences.

Make sense of it

An estimate and a measurement serve different purposes; both should name units and reasonable precision.

Keep wondering

Which object was hardest to estimate, and why?

Share your reasoning: Explain what you observed, what you think it means, and one thing this activity cannot establish. A surprising result or an honest “I don’t know yet” is useful evidence.

Make it work for you: Sketch, dictate to a trusted adult, or describe your evidence instead of writing. A partner can handle materials or measurements. If a step is inaccessible, discuss its prediction and clearly label it untested.

MAT-020Distance Rate Time

Family activity; adapt together · About 20–40 minutes; pause whenever useful

Begin with a question

How do speed and duration combine in a simple model?

Gather

Paper, pencil.

Before you begin: This is an invented model, not a prediction of vehicle travel.

Predict, explore, revise

  1. Imagine a toy traveling two grid units each second for five seconds.
  2. Draw each second's position and calculate total distance.
  3. Repeat at three units per second and explain the constant-speed assumption.

Keep your evidence

Two position tables and distance calculations.

Make sense of it

For constant speed, distance equals speed multiplied by time; real motion can vary.

Keep wondering

How would a two-second pause change the table?

Share your reasoning: Explain what you observed, what you think it means, and one thing this activity cannot establish. A surprising result or an honest “I don’t know yet” is useful evidence.

Make it work for you: Sketch, dictate to a trusted adult, or describe your evidence instead of writing. A partner can handle materials or measurements. If a step is inaccessible, discuss its prediction and clearly label it untested.

MAT-021Volume Layers

Family activity; adapt together · About 20–40 minutes; pause whenever useful

Begin with a question

How can layers help count a rectangular solid?

Gather

Large blocks or drawn cubes, paper.

Before you begin: Use blocks too large to swallow or work entirely on paper.

Predict, explore, revise

  1. Build or draw a base two units by three units.
  2. Add two identical layers above it for three layers total.
  3. Count directly and compare with length times width times height.

Keep your evidence

A labeled model and matching cube count.

Make sense of it

A rectangular prism's volume can be counted by equal layers; units are cubic.

Keep wondering

What changes if a cube is missing from one layer?

MAT-022Measurement Uncertainty

Family activity; adapt together · About 20–40 minutes; pause whenever useful

Begin with a question

Why might repeated measurements differ slightly?

Gather

Ruler, pencil, paper strip.

Before you begin: Use a safe ruler; no blades are required.

Predict, explore, revise

  1. Measure the same strip three times, resetting the ruler each time.
  2. Ask a partner to measure it without seeing your results.
  3. Compare the spread and discuss ruler markings and alignment.

Keep your evidence

Repeated measurements with units and a reasonable reported length.

Make sense of it

Measurement has limits; reporting many digits does not automatically make a result more accurate.

Keep wondering

What tool or method would reduce the largest uncertainty?

Share your reasoning: Explain what you observed, what you think it means, and one thing this activity cannot establish. A surprising result or an honest “I don’t know yet” is useful evidence.

MAT-023Budget a Model

Family activity; adapt together · About 20–40 minutes; pause whenever useful

Begin with a question

Can a design meet a fictional spending limit?

Gather

Paper, pencil, invented price list.

Before you begin: Use fictional tokens only; no purchasing or account information.

Predict, explore, revise

  1. Set a twenty-token budget with paper costing two, tape strips one and card pieces three.
  2. Plan a model and calculate the total.
  3. Create another feasible plan and compare remaining tokens.

Keep your evidence

Two itemized fictional budgets.

Make sense of it

A budget combines quantities and unit costs; the cheapest design may not meet the task requirements.

Keep wondering

What if one material's cost doubles?

Family activity; adapt together · About 20–40 minutes; pause whenever useful

Begin with a question

How many distinct outfits can we make from choices?

Gather

Paper, colored pencils.

Before you begin: Use drawings rather than buying or photographing clothing.

Independent choices multiply: each shirt can pair with each hat in this model.

MAT-025Evidence Review

Family activity; adapt together · About 20–40 minutes; pause whenever useful

Begin with a question

Which conclusion is justified by a small dataset?

Gather

Paper, pencil.

Before you begin: These are invented data for reasoning practice, not results from a tested product.

Predict, explore, revise

  1. Use fictional ramp distances 12, 14, 13 and 15 centimeters for design A, and 13, 13, 14 and 14 for B.
  2. Compare averages and ranges.
  3. Write a cautious conclusion and propose another trial rather than declaring a universal winner.

Keep your evidence

Calculations, a limited conclusion and a next-test plan.

Make sense of it

Similar averages can hide different spreads; a small dataset does not establish performance in all conditions.

Keep wondering

Which new condition would most challenge your conclusion?

Share your reasoning: Explain what you observed, what you think it means, and one thing this activity cannot establish. A surprising result or an honest “I don’t know yet” is useful evidence.

Make it work for you: Sketch, dictate to a trusted adult, or describe your evidence instead of writing. A partner can handle materials or measurements. If a step is inaccessible, discuss its prediction and clearly label it untested.
